Overview

Redesigning the online grocery shopping experience

FreshCart is an online grocery delivery platform serving over 200,000 monthly active users across 3 metropolitan areas. Despite strong product-market fit, the platform suffered from a high cart abandonment rate (68%) and low conversion from browse to purchase. The existing interface, built incrementally over 4 years, had accumulated significant design debt — inconsistent patterns, unclear navigation, and a checkout flow that frustrated users.

I was brought on as the lead UI designer to conduct a full redesign of the end-to-end shopping experience, from product discovery through checkout and order tracking. The goal was to modernize the visual language while fundamentally improving usability.

Background

Why the old design wasn't working

The original FreshCart interface was built by engineers during the company's startup phase. While functional, it lacked a cohesive design language. Key issues identified through stakeholder interviews and analytics review:

  • Inconsistent navigation: The category structure had grown organically to 140+ categories with no clear hierarchy, causing users to rely heavily on search.
  • Checkout friction: The 5-step checkout process required 23 form fields, with no guest checkout option. Hotjar recordings showed 42% of users dropping off at the address entry step.
  • Poor mobile experience: 65% of traffic was mobile, but the responsive layout was an afterthought — product cards were cramped, filters were hidden behind a tiny icon, and the cart overlay blocked the entire screen.
Discovery

Understanding user pain points

Before jumping into design, I led a 3-week discovery phase to build a solid foundation of user understanding.

User Interviews (12 participants)

I conducted moderated interviews with a mix of power users (weekly shoppers) and occasional users. Key findings:

  • Power users wanted faster repeat-ordering — they purchased the same 15-20 items weekly and found re-adding them tedious.
  • Occasional users struggled with product discovery — they often searched for generic terms like "snacks" and got overwhelmed by 200+ results with no meaningful filtering.
  • All participants expressed frustration with the delivery time slot picker, which showed slots in a flat list of 30+ options.

Heuristic Evaluation

I performed a heuristic evaluation against Nielsen's 10 usability heuristics and documented 34 issues, with 12 rated as severity 3 or above. The most critical issues centered around system status visibility (users couldn't tell if items were in stock until clicking into the detail page) and error prevention (the coupon field accepted invalid codes silently).

Competitive Analysis

I benchmarked against 5 competitor platforms (Instacart, Amazon Fresh, Ocado, Gorillas, Getir) across 8 dimensions. Key takeaway: all top performers featured a persistent mini-cart, in-line stock status, and a checkout flow of 3 steps or fewer.

Challenge 1

Simplifying the product discovery flow

The core problem: users couldn't efficiently find what they wanted. The category tree was too deep (up to 4 levels), and the search results page offered only basic text filtering.

Before

140+ flat categories in a mega-menu. No visual hierarchy. Search results showed a plain grid with no sort or filter beyond "relevance." Users averaged 4.2 clicks to reach a desired product.

After

Restructured into 8 top-level departments with visual icons, each expanding to 3-5 subcategories. Added smart filters (dietary, brand, price range, rating). Search now shows inline suggestions with product thumbnails. Average clicks to product: 2.1.

Design Decision: I introduced a "Quick Reorder" panel on the homepage for returning users, surfacing their last 3 orders as one-tap re-add lists. This reduced repeat-order time by 72% based on post-launch analytics.

Challenge 2

Redesigning the checkout experience

The original 5-step checkout was the single biggest drop-off point. Users had to create an account, enter a delivery address with manual formatting, choose from a confusing list of time slots, enter payment details, and review an order summary that didn't show delivery fees until the final step.

Solution: 3-Step Streamlined Checkout

I consolidated the flow into 3 clear steps with a persistent progress indicator:

  1. Delivery — Address auto-complete via Google Places API, saved addresses for returning users, and a visual calendar-style time slot picker replacing the flat list.
  2. Payment — Saved payment methods shown first, with Apple Pay and Google Pay as primary CTAs. New card entry uses a single-line card input pattern.
  3. Review & Place Order — Full cost breakdown (subtotal, delivery fee, service fee, discounts) visible from step 1 in a sticky sidebar, eliminating "surprise fees" at the end.
Before

5 steps, 23 form fields, no guest checkout, fees revealed only at step 5. Average completion time: 4m 38s. Abandonment at address step: 42%.

After

3 steps, 11 form fields (with auto-fill), guest checkout supported, sticky cost summary from step 1. Average completion time: 1m 52s. Abandonment at address step: 18%.

Challenge 3

Mobile-first responsive design

With 65% of traffic on mobile, I adopted a mobile-first design approach and created dedicated mobile patterns rather than simply reflowing the desktop layout.

Key Mobile Design Decisions

  • Bottom navigation bar: Moved primary navigation (Home, Search, Cart, Orders, Account) to a persistent bottom bar, following the thumb-zone principle for one-handed use.
  • Swipe interactions: Product cards in search results support swipe-right to add to cart and swipe-left to add to favorites — reducing the number of taps per action from 3 to 1.
  • Smart cart drawer: Instead of a full-screen cart overlay, implemented a bottom-sheet cart that shows item count and total, expandable to full height. Users can continue browsing while reviewing their cart.
  • Responsive grid: Desktop uses a 4-column product grid; tablet uses 3; mobile uses 2 columns with larger touch targets (minimum 48×48px).

Usability Testing Result: In a comparative test with 8 participants on mobile, the new design achieved a 96% task success rate for "find and add 5 items to cart" vs. 71% on the old design. Average time dropped from 3m 12s to 1m 28s.

Reflection

Lessons learned

  1. Show the cost early: The single biggest UX improvement was showing the fee breakdown from step 1. Users hate surprises at checkout — transparency builds trust.
  2. Design for the repeat user: In grocery e-commerce, most revenue comes from repeat orders. Investing in "Quick Reorder" was a high-leverage feature that initially wasn't on the product roadmap.
  3. Mobile isn't a shrunk desktop: Dedicated mobile interactions (swipe, bottom sheet, bottom nav) outperformed simply reflowing the desktop layout. The extra design effort paid off in dramatically better mobile metrics.
